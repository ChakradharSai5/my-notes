Entropy became important in this discussion because it explains something that energy conservation alone cannot explain:

> **Why can energy be conserved while useful work potential is lost?**

A throttling valve is a perfect example.

Across an ideal throttle:

- enthalpy remains approximately constant
- entropy increases

So the energy has **not disappeared**.

Instead, the process has become more irreversible.

The fluid after throttling has **less ability to produce useful mechanical work** in a subsequent process.

This is what "throttling loss" really means.

It does **not** mean:

> "Energy has been destroyed."

It means:

> **Some of the opportunity to convert the fluid's energy into useful work has been lost because the process generated entropy.**

---

### Turbine connection

Consider two cases.

#### Case A — pressure drop occurs in the turbine

The turbine can extract work from the expansion.

#### Case B — pressure drop occurs across the control valve first

The valve generates entropy without producing useful shaft work.

The steam then enters the turbine in a degraded thermodynamic state.

Therefore the turbine extracts less useful work than it could have extracted without the unnecessary throttling.

So:

> **Pressure drop across a turbine can produce work.**

> **Pressure drop across a throttle produces no useful shaft work and generates entropy.**

That is the core idea behind **throttling loss**.
