For a pure substance, the liquid-vapor region needs special treatment.

At a fixed pressure, there is a specific temperature at which liquid and vapor can coexist. This is the **saturation condition**.

There are three important states around the liquid-vapor transition:

### Saturated liquid

The substance is entirely liquid, right at the point where vaporization is about to begin.

Quality:

`x = 0`

### Two-phase mixture

Liquid and vapor coexist.

`0 < x < 1`

Quality tells us how much of the total mass is vapor.

For example:

- `x = 0` → 100% liquid
- `x = 0.2` → 20% vapor, 80% liquid
- `x = 0.5` → 50% vapor
- `x = 1` → 100% vapor

### Saturated vapor

The substance is entirely vapor, right at the point where condensation would begin.

Quality:

`x = 1`

After this, adding more heat produces **superheated vapor**.

---

### Why P and T sometimes stop being enough

In a single-phase region, pressure and temperature can independently locate the state.

But during liquid-vapor equilibrium, pressure and temperature become linked by the saturation condition.

For example:

At a given pressure, the saturation temperature is already fixed.

Therefore:

`P → Tsat`

or:

`T → Psat`

So specifying both P and T does not tell us **how much liquid and vapor are present**.

For that, we need another property such as:

- quality
- enthalpy
- entropy
- specific volume

This is why a state inside the two-phase region can require something like:

`P + x`

or:

`P + h`

to determine the exact state.

This is a special case of the broader idea:

> **Two independent properties define the state, but P and T are not independent inside the liquid-vapor equilibrium condition.**
