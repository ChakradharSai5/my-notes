A thermodynamic **state** is the complete condition of a substance at a particular instant/equilibrium condition.

There are many properties that can describe the state:

- Pressure
- Temperature
- Enthalpy
- Entropy
- Specific volume
- Internal energy
- Density
- Quality (in a two-phase mixture)

But these are **not all independent**.
For a **simple pure substance in a ==*single phase*==*,* only **two independent intensive properties** are required to completely define the thermodynamic state.

A point in a plane can be represented by:

- `(x, y)`
- or `(r, θ)`

They are different coordinate systems describing the same point.

Similarly, a thermodynamic state can be represented by different pairs of independent properties.

For example:

- Pressure + Temperature
- Pressure + Enthalpy
- Pressure + Entropy
- Enthalpy + Entropy
- etc.
Once the two independent properties are known, the other thermodynamic properties are determined by the physical properties of that substance.

For example, conceptually:

`h = f(P,T)`

`s = f(P,T)`

`v = f(P,T)`

This does **not** mean that every possible pair of properties is always independent.

The key rule is:

> **Two independent properties determine the state.**
