**Throttling** is the process of forcing a fluid through a restriction such as a valve.
**THE VALVE THROTTLING LOSSES IS THE ENTROY LOSS, NOT THE MW LOSS, BECAUSE MW LOSS IS HAPPENING WITH STEAM FLOW REDUCTION.**
The defining ideal behavior is:

> **Pressure drops while enthalpy remains approximately constant.**

The valve does not primarily remove heat from the fluid.

It does not "consume" the missing energy.

It does not need to become hot because the fluid became colder.

Instead, the pressure changes and the fluid moves toward the equilibrium state corresponding to the new pressure.

Because the process is irreversible, entropy increases.

So the defining combination is:

> **Pressure decreases + enthalpy approximately constant + entropy increases.**

### Refrigeration example

A high-pressure liquid refrigerant enters the expansion valve.

The valve drops its pressure.

At the new low pressure, the old state is no longer the equilibrium state.

Some liquid therefore flashes into vapor, producing a cold liquid-vapor mixture.

The exact amount of vapor depends on:

- the inlet state
- the outlet pressure
- the thermodynamic properties of the refrigerant

This cold mixture then enters the evaporator and absorbs heat from the room.

The valve itself is therefore **preparing the refrigerant for heat absorption**.

### Steam turbine example

The turbine governing/control valve is also a throttling device.

Steam enters at high pressure and is throttled to a lower pressure before entering the turbine.

Again:

- pressure decreases
- enthalpy is approximately unchanged
- entropy increases

But unlike the refrigeration case, the pressure drop across the turbine control valve is generally **thermodynamically undesirable**.

Why?

Because if that pressure difference were used inside the turbine, the turbine could convert some of it into shaft work.

Across the valve, essentially no useful shaft work is produced.

So throttling is useful for **control**, but creates a thermodynamic penalty.
